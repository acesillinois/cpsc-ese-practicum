## Greenhouse studies of Amaranthus phenotypes in response to competition
<p>
The genus <it>Amaranthus</it> includes approximately 75 species, some of which are global agricultural
weeds, rural weeds, cultivated grain crops, and leafy vegetable crops. This makes the genus
Amaranthus an adequate study system for understanding the evolution of * agricultural weeds *.
</p>
<p>
Innumerable species are affected by rapid environmental change, particularly due to agriculture.
One of the conditions imposed by agriculture is competition, with crops, most of which in recent
years are engineered to be superior in human-controlled environments.
Baker (1974) has mentioned several ideal weed characteristics, one of which is plastic growth in
a wide range of environmental conditions. Previous studies (Kreiner, 2021; Waselkov 2014 &
2020) have focused on individual species, particularly Palmer amaranth, waterhemp, and grain
Amaranth, with limited comparative studies among the species within the genus. The current
knowledge gap is whether weedy and cultivated Amaranthus species will exhibit similar
plasticity under this competitive environment in agriculture.
</p>
<p>
  To address this challenge, we selected 39 species of the genus Amaranthus with different origins,
available through USDA-ARS Germplasm Resources Information Network (GRIN). We will
conduct a greenhouse experiment comparing the traits of each group, global and rural weedy
with cultivated Amaranthus, under standardized environmental conditions. Each species will
have 4 controls with one accession each pot and 2 competition treatments with 4 accessions and
a soybean on each pot. We will compare the architectural traits, differences between species in
the form of plant height, stem thickness, branching, and canopy area in several stage of growth.
We want to understand how Amaranthus evolves into these diverse growth strategies in response
to competition, but first we need to understand what differentiates weedy from cultivated
Amaranthus. 
</p> 
<p>
  The results will improve our understanding of traits associated with weediness,
with potentially allowing us to delve further into the future of weed management strategies.
</p>
